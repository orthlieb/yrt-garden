# Vitre

*Town · Claimed by Nysis and Mososi, held by neither · **Settled** · Glass, and nothing else · pop. ~700 · off-oracle*

**Exports** — Glass. Bottles, jars and apothecary ware by the crate; crown quarries for leaded windows; lamp chimneys, and the thick green rounds that go into a ship's deck to light the hold. And out of four chairs in thirty, work with no competitor anywhere in Yrt: clear metal thin enough to read a page through, canes twisted into the wall of a cup, opaque white laid against colourless, lead glass that is heavy in the hand and rings when it is struck and takes a cut edge.

**Imports** — Everything, because Vitre grows nothing and digs nothing. Quartz and lime rubble carted off the [[Pinna Mtns]] slag banks. Coal and fireclay out of [[Termin]]. Soda ash from the [[Seka Lands]]. Litharge from the [[Oray Hills]], by barge and then by wagon. Grain, cloth, salt, beer, and every mouthful anyone in the town eats.

Between the Mososi customs post at [[Termin]] and the Nysisian one a day's walk east lies a strip of ground that neither country has ever been able to hand to the other. The border cairns run through it, some of them moved. Both governments assess duty at their own end and neither assesses anything in the middle, because to tax the middle is to say the middle is yours, and to say that out loud is to invite the argument nobody has wanted for two hundred years.

Vitre sits in that gap, and it is on fire more or less continuously.

You smell it before the road tops the rise: coal smoke laid flat over the ground on a still day, and under it something sharp and mineral that people who have not been here cannot place. Then the works themselves — long low sheds with tall stacks, spoil banks of white ground rock, wagon yards, cooling sheds, and the glow at night off six furnaces that have not been allowed to go out in eleven years.

## The Strip

Nothing about Vitre's freedom was granted. It is a by-product of two clerks who are each forbidden to concede a point.

Mososi's *prefekto* cannot license the works, because a licence is a claim, and the moment Mososi claims the strip, Nysis has a grievance it can take to the crossing — and the crossing is worth more to Mososi than every pane of glass ever blown here. Nysis's *urbestro* is under exactly the same constraint from the other direction, and being answerable to nobody, has even less appetite for making a decision that outlives him. So neither taxes Vitre, neither inspects it, neither writes its name in a ledger. The paperwork that follows Vitre glass into either country describes it as goods *presented at the post* and is careful never to say where they were made. Both offices know. Both offices have found that knowing is cheaper than recording.

What this buys the works is a total absence of law. What it costs the people in it is a country.

A man born in Vitre is not a subject of anywhere. He is not forbidden to leave — no one has the standing to forbid him anything — but a day's walk in either direction puts him at a post, with no birth entered anywhere, no urbestro's mark, no prefekto's roll, holding a trade that both governments would enormously like to have explained to them at length. Some go anyway. Freeport will take anyone. But Freeport is a long way, and the first hundred yards of the journey are the ones that require deciding you are a person who can be stopped and questioned, which is not a thing anyone raised here has ever been.

Nobody built this cage. It assembled itself out of two men being correct with each other.

## The Works

A glass furnace is a decision you cannot revisit. Bringing one to working heat takes days of banked fire, and if you let it fall the pots crack as they cool and the whole thing must be dug out and rebuilt. So it does not fall. The furnaces run day and night through a campaign of eight or nine months, worked in shifts around the clock, and Vitre's year is shaped by that fact the way a farming year is shaped by frost.

Coal is why the town is here rather than somewhere with better sand, and coal is also the problem, because coal smoke will ruin a melt — it puts sulphur in the metal and turns it the colour of weak tea. The answer, and the first of the things Vitre does not discuss, is that the pots are lidded. The glass never sees the flame. Those pots are thrown from fireclay dug out from under Termin's coal seams, and how they are wedged and fired and how long they are left to season is the property of two families who have never written any of it down.

Underfoot the whole town is white with rock dust. Quartz comes in as gangue off the Pinna banks — waste, worth nothing to a smelter, which is the only reason a works could ever afford it in this quantity — and goes under water-driven stamps until it is flour. Calcite from the same banks gives the lime. Soda comes from the Seka, where saltwort burned on the pans yields an ash the clans have sold for two generations without ever being told what it is for.

Work is organised around the **chair**, which is a bench and also a rank. The man who holds it shapes; a servitor takes and carries and does the second half of everything; a boy gathers metal on the pipe and sweats and watches. You do not get a chair for being good. You get one when the man who had it dies or his hands stop, after twenty years of standing beside him doing the part you are not permitted to do. There are thirty chairs in Vitre. There is no other authority in the town, and there does not need to be, because the furnace settles most arguments before anyone raises his voice: the metal is ready when it is ready, and it does not care whose father was whose.

## What Comes Out

The bulk trade pays for the town. Bottles, jars, quarries, chimneys — green and brown and full of bubbles, made fast in open pots by chairs that will never do anything else, and sold by the crate to people who would be astonished to be told the same works made anything remarkable.

Then there is the other thing.

Clear glass is difficult because iron is everywhere, and iron makes glass green. The Vitre answer has four parts, and each part on its own is unremarkable, which is exactly why the secret has held. The quartz is picked — white cobbles only, sorted by hand out of the Pinna waste by women paid by weight, because vein quartz carries almost no iron where sand carries a great deal. The pots are lidded. The flux is Seka soda rather than wood ash. And into the batch goes a measure of a soft black ore that comes over on the same wagons as the quartz, and which the Pinna throws away with everything else it cannot smelt. It cancels the green. In Vitre it is called **soap**, and men who have worked here forty years could not tell you what is in it, only how much and when.

What comes off those four chairs is sold in [[Freeport]] at prices that would fund a small war, and Vitre sees a fraction of it, because Vitre cannot sell anything itself. It has no factor, no broker, no standing to sign a contract, and no court anywhere that would hear it if a contract were broken. The ware leaves as bonded freight and the margin is made four hundred miles away by men who have never seen a furnace.

## The Cold Weeks

Every campaign ends. The fires are let down, the pots are dug out and replaced, the stacks are relined, and for five or six weeks in the worst of the summer heat there is no work in Vitre at all.

The town empties. Families walk out to relatives in Termin and the Mososi villages, to Fluenti on the barges, to wherever anyone still has kin who will take them, and for a month the strip holds a few hundred people minding the sheds and quarrelling about pot clay. Then the fires go back up and, one by one, everyone comes back.

Almost everyone. The cold weeks are when Vitre loses people, and both customs posts know it, and both posts spend those weeks doing rather more careful work than usual on the papers of anyone travelling in company with a man whose hands are scarred across the backs and who cannot say where he was born.

**Features**
- Six stacks smoking over a treeless flat, visible from a half-day out
- White rock dust in every seam of every garment, and in the bread
- The stamps going day and night, felt through the ground before it is heard
- Furnace mouths bricked to a slot, and the orange bar of light that comes out of them
- Cooling sheds where the ware crawls the length of the building over days
- Cullet heaps sorted by colour, picked over by children
- Spoil banks of white quartz, and the sorting women working them by weight
- Wagons arriving loaded with rock and leaving loaded with straw and crates
- Thirty chairs, and the exact order of them known to every child in the town
- Border cairns running through the middle of the yards, whitewashed by somebody every spring
- No temple, no watch house, no gate, and nowhere to register a birth
- The five weeks in summer when the fires are down and half the houses are shut

**Quest Starter**
> A Vitre gaffer wants to leave with his family and cannot, and has worked out that he does not need permission — he needs to be a citizen of somewhere before he reaches a post. He will pay in the only currency he has, which is a crate of ware worth more than the escort will earn in a decade, and he wants it done in the cold weeks when the road is thick with people who look exactly like him. What he has not said is that two families own the pot clay, that he is not of either, and that they have already worked out what he is worth to a country that does not yet have a glassworks.

**Quest Starter**
> Both customs posts have received the same instruction from their capitals within a month of each other: establish, discreetly, how many people live in the strip. Neither knows the other has it. Nobody in Vitre has ever been counted, there is no register to consult, and the only honest way to do it is to walk in and ask — which is the one act that would tell seven hundred stateless people exactly what is being decided about them, and empty the town in a week.
