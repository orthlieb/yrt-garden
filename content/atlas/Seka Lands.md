# Seka Lands

*Boundary · Mososi · Dry grazing, salt, hides; clan law and no other kind*

**Region:** Seka Lands · **Location:** Plain
**Location detail:** South-west of Lake Mater, beyond the Polvokovrita Gap, where the rain stops

South-west of Lake Mater the rain stops. Whatever the [[Polvokovrita Gap]] does to the weather coming off the water, the country beyond it gets almost none of it, and the Seka is the result: a wide, pale, open landscape of thorn scrub, wiry grass, dust, and dry watercourses that run three times a year and are the most dangerous thing in the region when they do. The horizon is very far away. There is nothing between you and it.

People live here and always have, and they are not enduring it. They are extremely good at it, and they have been for longer than Mososi has been a country.

## The Cycle

The Seka clans work goats, sheep, and a small tough breed of cattle across ranges that would swallow a Nyssian county, and the thing outsiders never grasp is the timescale. This is not transhumance: up in summer, down in winter, home again. A clan's circuit is measured in **years**: a named sequence of grounds, each grazed once and then left for three or four seasons to come back, walked in an order that has been fixed for generations and is adjusted only under duress. A child born on one ground may be twelve before the clan stands on it again and will have heard about it the whole time.

The herd is arranged the same way, by function rather than preference. Goats browse thorn and will find something to eat in a year that kills everything else; they are the clan's insurance and nobody sentimentalises them. Sheep take the grass and carry the wool and hides that go out. The cattle are few, they drink more than they are worth, and they are kept anyway, because cattle are how a clan is counted: a herd is a ledger, a dowry, a history and an argument all at once, and a man who cannot recite four generations of a line of cows is not somebody whose word carries.

What people actually live on is milk, and curd, and dried curd that keeps for months and can be carried. Meat is a rare thing and mostly means something has died or something is being marked. Killing stock to eat is spending capital, and the Seka do not have any other kind: no granary, no cellar, nothing stored anywhere that does not walk. That is the strength of the system and it is also the whole of its fragility. A clan that loses its herd has not lost its income. It has stopped existing, and its people are absorbed by relatives within the year and their circuit is redistributed, and this happens perhaps twice in a generation and is spoken about in the flat tone people use for weather.

## Water

Every law in the Seka is a water law and there are no others worth the name.

**Digging is title.** Whoever sank a well holds it, and their descendants hold it after them, and that holding does not lapse through disuse or distance: a clan that has not stood at a particular well in nine years still owns it, and everyone knows they do, and the mark cut into the rock beside it says whose it is in a script every Seka child can read. The marks are the whole register of property in this country. There is no other document anywhere.

Around that sits an etiquette that visitors find either beautiful or insane. A stranger's animals must be watered (refusing is not done, and a clan that refused would find the story ahead of it for years), but watering is not permission to stay, and the distinction is exact and is not explained twice. Order of drawing is fixed: the holder's stock, then guests, then whoever else is present, and a man who takes out of turn at a well in a dry year has done something his own people will settle before the injured party has to. Fouling a source is the one act with no procedure attached to it at all, because it has never been treated as a dispute.

And then there is the other water. The dry courses run three times a year, and they run from rain that fell somewhere else, often over hills a full day away, out of a sky that is clear where you are standing. What arrives is a wall of brown water in a channel that was dust an hour ago, and it takes tents, stock, and people who were doing nothing wrong. Every Seka knows the sound of one filling before they can see it, and the standing instruction given to children is not *run* but *go up*, and it is given before they can walk.

## Salt

South, in the low country, the pans lie white and dead flat and are the Seka's one export that the rest of Mososi genuinely needs.

The work is simple and brutal: the crust is scored and lifted in slabs, trimmed square, stacked to dry hard, and lashed in pairs onto donkeys for a train that goes out strung a mile long. Nobody owns the pans. The right to cut is held the same way a well is held, by mark and by memory, and the cutting seasons are staggered between clans by an arrangement nobody has ever written down and nobody has broken in living memory.

The pans yield a second thing that the clans regard as a by-product and price accordingly. The saltwort that grows on the sour ground around them is cut, dried and burned, and the grey ash raked out of the pit is sold by the sack to a buyer who comes twice a year and does not explain himself. It goes to [[Vitre]], where it is half of what makes glass clear. The clans know it is worth more than they get. They have never been able to find out how much more, because the only people who could tell them are four days' travel away and stateless.

What makes salt matter beyond the table is that out here it *is* money. A trimmed slab of a standard size is a known quantity (it does not spoil, it does not need an assayer, and it can be broken for change), and in the western reaches, where coin is thin and a Freeport gent means very little, prices are quoted in slabs and settled in slabs. The clans consider [[Materton]]'s merchants to be children at this. They are correct, and the merchants know it, and the exchange rate between salt and silver is negotiated fresh at every meeting by people who have been negotiating it their whole lives against people who do it twice a year.

## What Mososi Gets

Mososi claims the Seka and does not administer it, and both sides find this ideal.

The Imperiestro's writ is acknowledged in principle, ignored in practice, and invoked selectively by whichever clan is currently losing an argument, which is the only circumstance in which anybody out here has ever asked for an official. What the empire gets is salt, hides, wool, and a western march that costs nothing to hold because the people on it are not going anywhere and have no interest whatever in Mososi's affairs. What it would cost to actually govern is more than the Seka has ever produced. Nobody in [[Typpe]] has proposed it in two generations.

The clans reckon a person by lineage and by herd, and the Puritan caste grading of the north (Pure, Prime, Second, Third) reads to them as a foreign obsession with facts that do not bear on anything. This is not tolerance and should not be mistaken for it. It is that they have looked at the question and found it uninteresting.

**Features**
- Thorn scrub and wiry grass to a very distant horizon
- Dry watercourses, and the sound of one filling before you see it
- Dust that gets into food, water, eyes, and stays
- Herds on a circuit of years, and grounds not stood on since childhood
- Cattle counted, recited, and worth less than they cost
- Salt pans in the low south, scored, lifted and stacked white
- Donkey trains strung out for a mile
- Clan marks cut into rock at every water source, and read by everyone
- Wells, and the extremely serious etiquette around them
- Prices quoted in slabs, and settled in them
- Heat shimmer, and things in it that are not there
- No imperial presence of any kind

**Quest Starter**
> A well that four clans have shared for six generations has been fouled. Not dried, fouled, deliberately and with something that will not wash out. The dry season has eight weeks left. Every clan knows what the accusation will be, and every clan knows what follows the accusation.
