Oray Hills

Boundary · Nysis · Upland grazing, wool, transhumance; silver, lead, gold

Region: Oray Hills · Location: Hill Location detail: North and west of Fluenti, past the last of the cut

North and west of Fluenti, past the last of the cut, the land stops being forest and becomes hill. The Oray is high open ground (bracken, heather, cropped turf, wind) rolling away in long grey-green swells with the bones of the rock showing through at the crests. There are no towns. There are shielings: low stone huts with turf roofs, occupied from late spring to first frost and empty the rest of the year, and a network of drove roads worn a foot deep into the ground by four generations of hooves.

The Oray families come up from the lake shore each year with sheep and hardy little cattle and they come down again before the weather turns, and in between they are entirely on their own. It is not dangerous country in the way the mountains are dangerous. It is unattended country. If you break a leg on the Oray in high summer you may be found in a day; you may be found in a month. The herders’ etiquette around this is elaborate and taken seriously: the smoke signals, the cairn marks, the standing obligation to check an unlit shieling.

The hills are also where the region’s oldest visible things are. Standing stones, some of them pre-Fall survey markers and some older; long low earthworks that do not enclose anything; and in three places the levelled rectangular footprints of Unmade structures, robbed out for building stone over two centuries and still legible in the turf. The herders use them as landmarks and navigate by them and have their own names for them, and no scholar has ever come up here to ask what those names are.

And under all of it is the metal, which is going to end everything described above.

What Is In The Rock

The gold came first because gold always does. It is in the burns, in the gravels, and it has been panned casually by herders’ children for as long as anyone has been coming up here: a few grains a season, a nuisance-hobby, nothing that changed a life. It is also not gold, quite. Oray gold comes out pale, because it is alloyed in the ground with silver, and a nugget off these hills runs somewhere between a fifth and a quarter silver before anybody touches it. That is normal and it is what gold is nearly everywhere; it just means the assayer’s answer is never the number the finder expected.

The gold is not the prize. The prize is that this is lead country, and the lead carries silver.

The veins run through the hill rock in the crests and the valley sides: heavy grey ore, bright on a fresh break, absurdly dense in the hand, and shot through with silver in quantities that are worthless per ton and enormous per hillside. Alongside it in the same veins: zinc, which nobody here can use and which goes on the spoil; copper in patches; and, in the eastern workings, an ore that carries arsenic, which is why the men on that side get sick in a particular way that the men on the western side do not.

Nobody found this recently. Everybody knew there was lead. What changed is that lead stopped being the point.

Cupellation

Getting silver out of lead is old, slow, filthy, and completely reliable, and the whole industrial character of the Oray follows from the process.

The ore is smelted down to lead bullion. The bullion is then run into a cupel (a shallow hearth lined with bone ash, packed and smoothed by men whose only job that is) and blown with a hard draught at heat for hours. The lead oxidises. The bone ash drinks the oxide. What is left in the middle of the hearth when it is done, sitting in a dish of grey exhausted ash, is a button of silver.

The oxide is not wasted; it is skimmed as litharge and goes east to the glass sheds at Vitre, where it makes the heavy ware that rings, and comes back to the Oray eventually as somebody’s window. The silver goes south under escort. And where the gold and silver have come out of the ground married to each other, they are parted with salt and heat in a separate operation that is jealously held by two families and is the only step in the whole business that anyone bothers to keep secret.

All of it needs fuel and all of it needs it constantly. There is no forest up here. What there is, in enormous quantity, is peat, so the smelt is fed by peat cut from the hill itself, in banks that are now visible for miles, on ground that will not carry a sheep again in the lifetime of anyone reading this.

The Grass and the Smelt

The smoke kills the pasture. This is not disputed, not by the herders and not by the smelter men, and there is no version of the industry that avoids it.

Downwind of a working smelt the turf takes lead, and stock grazing that turf takes it in turn, and the result is what the Oray families call the staggers: an animal that goes blind, grinds its teeth, walks in a way that is wrong before it is obviously wrong, and dies. Lambs first. It arrives a valley at a time. A family can lose the work of four generations in two summers and be entirely unable to name the day it started.

So there are two economies in these hills and only one set of hills. The herders have four generations of drove roads, an obligation system, and no charter. The workings have a charter, wages, and a smelt house that runs all winter.

That last part is the strangest thing on the Oray now and the thing the old people cannot get used to. These hills were empty from first frost to late spring for as long as there have been Oray families. There are fires up here in January.

Charters and Coin

There is no authority on the Oray, so the charters are sold from the lake shore. Fluenti’s urbestro issues them, takes the fee, and has therefore been selling licences to poison the pasture of the families who elect him, which he does not put that way, and which is understood well enough that nobody who wants a quiet life says it in front of him either.

What makes the arrangement immovable is where the silver goes. It goes south to Freeport, to the Mint, and comes out stamped as gents, and a good part of the coin in every purse in the region began as an Oray hillside. The Conclave lays its signature on each coin as it leaves the press and is paid for doing so, which means the order has a standing interest in a trade it has nothing to do with. Between a mint, a mayor, a chartered company and an order taking a fee on every piece struck, the number of people with a reason to keep the smelt houses burning is large, and the number with a reason to care about a valley of dead lambs is one family at a time.

Features

  • Long open swells of heather and cropped turf, and peat banks cut visible for miles
  • Wind that does not stop, and no shelter from it
  • Shielings, empty and locked, most of the year
  • Drove roads worn a foot into the ground
  • Cairn marks and smoke signals between hills
  • Standing stones on the skyline, and new workings robbing them for stone
  • Robbed-out Unmade footprints, legible in the grass
  • Burns with children panning in them out of habit, for pale gold
  • Grey ore bright on a fresh break, and far heavier than it looks
  • Bone-ash cupels, and the smell of a hearth blown hard for six hours
  • Smelt smoke lying downhill in still weather, and the grass under it
  • Lights on the hills in January, where there were never lights before

Quest Starter

A shieling has been unlit for six days in high summer and the family that should be in it has not come down. The herders’ obligation says someone must go and look. What is found there is not an accident, and the Oray has no watch, no magistrate, and no one to hand it to.

Second Quest Starter

An Oray family has lost eighty head to the staggers in two summers and has worked out, correctly, which smelt is upwind of them. They cannot sue, because there is no court up here and the charter was issued at the lake. They have asked for help getting a hearing at Fluenti. And separately, and without saying so in the same room, two of the sons have been pricing lamp oil.
