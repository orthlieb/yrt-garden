Language in Yrt

Language in Yrt

Sep 05, 202612 min read

Premise

The Unmaking (Year 0 SU = 2070 in old reckoning) collapsed an industrial, mass-literate, globally-connected society into a fragmented, subsistence-economy, regionally-isolated medieval-to-early-renaissance condition. Two hundred and thirty years later, the language people speak has drifted, significantly in some registers, conservatively in others.

This document covers the linguistic state of Yrt at the novel’s present (230 SU = 2300 old reckoning), what has drifted, what has been preserved, and how this should shape prose written about and within the world.

See also: Timeline to Present (calendar), Refiners Guild (working trade vocabulary), The Conclave (institutional language), Religion (liturgical register).

Historical Comparators

A 230-year drift period, with collapse from high technology to medieval/early-modern conditions, maps best onto two real historical episodes:

The fall of Rome (476 AD → ~700 AD = ~225 years)

Classical Latin began breaking into proto-Romance dialects within this span. Local speech diverged sharply. Greek philosophy and Roman technical knowledge survived only in monasteries; most people became illiterate; engineering vocabulary (aqueducts, concrete formulations, glassmaking specialties) was lost or attenuated. But the Church preserved Latin as a working language for liturgy, law, and learning. By 700 AD, an educated cleric could still read Cicero with effort; a peasant could not understand the cleric’s speech.

This is the best single comparator for Yrt’s situation. Institutional continuity (Conclave, Pura Ecclesia, Brotherhood) parallels the Church’s role; the fragmented common-speech parallels the Romance dialects’ emergence; the technical-vocabulary loss is similar in scope.

Late Middle English to Early Modern English (~1400 → ~1600 = 200 years)

Chaucer to Shakespeare. Massive shifts in pronunciation (the Great Vowel Shift), vocabulary, and grammar. Standardization began only with the printing press (~1450) and stable spelling came much later. This comparator illustrates how dramatic phonological and grammatical drift can be inside a single 200-year span without external catastrophe: pure cumulative generational change.

Why English 1795 → 2025 (230 years) is the wrong comparator

Modern English has industrial stability, mass literacy, mass media, dictionaries, and continuous standardization. Yrt has none of these post the Unmaking. Modern English’s near-stability over 230 years is the exception, not the rule.

Forces of Drift in Yrt

  • Loss of standardization. No mass schooling, no dictionaries, no broadcast media. Each region’s speech diverges based on local conditions.
  • Loss of contact across distances. Travel takes weeks. Freeport doesn’t hear from Pinna mining communities or distant inland regions on any timescale that would homogenize speech.
  • Generational turnover compounded by population collapse. The Unmaking killed most speakers of the pre-Fall language. Survivors taught their children a simplified, locally-flavored version. Each successive generation drifts further.
  • Loss of technical vocabulary as the things go away. Words for pre-Fall electronics, computing, biology, mass transportation, mass medicine: gone, because the things they named are gone.
  • Working trade-specific vocabulary fills gaps. New industries (mana refinement, blight management, Touched social categorization) generate their own slang, which then becomes the operational dialect of those who work in them.
  • No formal grammar instruction. Working-class speech simplifies constructions, drops irregular verbs, contracts, locally invents idioms.

Forces of Continuity

  • The Conclave. A literate, institutional, multi-generational organization that conserves vocabulary deliberately. Manite, Veil, Amber, Compulsion, Sustained-channel, Disciple, Adept, Maester, Arcanist: these terms have been formally taught from teacher to student for centuries. Conclave-trained speakers across Yrt would mostly understand each other in technical matters even when their common speech diverged.
  • The Pura Ecclesia. Religious institutions are linguistically conservative. Liturgical phrases, ritual formulas, scriptural language. Sermons in 230 SU would use grammatical constructions and vocabulary the average peasant might find archaic but not unintelligible, and the persistence of religious language pulls everyday speech back toward the older forms.
  • The Refiners’ Guild. Its founding charter (142 SU, the Year of the Black Rain) and its operational documents preserve working-class trade vocabulary across decades. Goo, hard steam, brass crowns, troughs, drift: Brusc Halvert’s tour (215 SU) demonstrates the Guild’s working dialect.
  • The Town Watch and the merchant guilds. Institutional discipline, daily routines, training that uses formal vocabulary. Dux, Deputy, watch team, station team, customs chit, runner. Working-class but institutional.
  • Books in wealthy households. Estate libraries, the Brotherhood’s commercial records, the Conclave archives. Educated children grow up reading pre-Fall and immediate-post-Fall material. The literate class is small (probably 5-10% of Freeport’s population) but it preserves continuity for the rest.
  • Stable place names for old geographic features. Falter Bay, Falter Sound, the Pinna Mountains, Collima, Ravenna, the Duforq River. These names predate the Unmaking and have been used continuously.

What Has Drifted (Examples Already in Canon)

Several drifts are visible in the existing worldbuilding and serve as evidence of where Yrt’s language has settled:

“Mana” from “Manite”

The canonical example. Quantum Applied Materials’ commercial trademark Manite™ survives in Conclave-technical contexts; the folk-shortened mana is the everyday word. An educated character distinguishes the two; a working laborer might not. This is the stratification pattern the Roman-fall comparator predicts: institutional preservation of the formal term, common drift toward the colloquial.

”The Unmade” as a proper noun

Almost certainly derived from a longer pre-Fall construction (the Unmade-era technologies, the Unmade society) that got compressed into a noun for the people themselves. The pre-Fall civilization has become a folk-historical category.

”The Veil”

Originally a technical description of the Amber-relay procedure (a veil of relays descends around the initiate’s head per The Conclave.md) that has become both the ceremonial name for the binding rite and the conceptual name for the Amber implant itself. Religious-ceremonial language preserved through institutional teaching.

”Touched”

Drifted from a clinical-medical descriptor of QAMed-Azure-altered humanity into a religious-folkloric term carrying social weight (Pure, Prime, Second, Third, Feral as a caste system). The original medical meaning is gone from common use; the religious meaning is what survives. The caste vocabulary itself (Pure, Prime, etc.) is a post-Unmaking institutional invention.

Year-names instead of dates

The Year of the Long Frost, The Year of the Black Rain, The Year the Walls Wept. Most cultures with universal literacy use numerical years; cultures with limited literacy and oral folk-memory use named years. Yrt’s calendar reflects the literacy stratification: the educated use 215 SU; the working class uses the Long Frost. Both systems coexist and serve different registers.

”Mermaid” as Freeport’s heraldry

Probably an Old Earth myth that survived as a maritime symbol. Heraldic conventions (Azure, a mermaid argent stantant facing sinister) are themselves a continuous medieval European institutional tradition that the worldbuilding has consciously preserved. Symbolic and heraldic vocabulary is among the most conservative registers.

Color-name codification

Gray, Azure, Crimson, Verdant, Luminous, Amber, Black are originally QAM’s product-line color codes. They have been preserved technically through the Conclave’s continuous use and have become folk-vocabulary for the manite types. A Conclave Maester says Verdant; a farmer probably says green mana.

What Has Been Lost or Mythologized

  • Pre-Fall scientific terminology. Quantum mechanics, neurology, electromagnetic theory, biochemistry, computing. These have no native speakers. Some terms survive in Conclave-Alchemist research as obscure technical jargon Saska Tristan would use but no one else would.
  • Pre-Fall geography beyond local features. Continents, countries, cities other than Yrt’s named ones. Whatever the Unmaking’s world was called isn’t named in current speech. The old maps may exist in Saska’s hidden panel; folk-memory does not name what is on them.
  • Modern political vocabulary. Democracy, parliament, federalism, citizen rights, judicial review, separation of powers. The Triumvirate’s political language is its own thing, not derived from modern democratic terminology.
  • Modern social science. Sociology, psychology, anthropology as disciplines and their vocabularies. Yrt has no clinical terminology for a disordered mind; the closest the Conclave gets is mis-seating and drift, which are working-class observations rather than diagnoses.
  • Modern entertainment vocabulary. Films, television, music genres as a system, popular fiction conventions. Yrt’s entertainment is oral storytelling, ballads, tavern songs, festival drama, religious pageant. The vocabulary follows.
  • Specific Unmade institutions. The Institute for Programmable Matter Studies (the body that authored the Manite Introduction document in 2041 = Year 29 BU) exists as a name on an old document; nobody at 230 SU could explain what an institute or a study in the modern sense was. Both words have survived but with shifted meanings (institute now means a Conclave or Ecclesia body; study now means private contemplation or research).
  • Mass-communication vocabulary. Newspapers, broadcasts, internet, social media. Lost without exception.
  • Industrial standardization vocabulary. SI units, ISO standards, brand names, model numbers. Some weights and measures survive (pounds, feet, miles); precision measurement vocabulary largely does not.

What Has Been Coined Post-Unmaking

  • Working-class trade slang of the refinement industry. Brusc Halvert’s tour demonstrates this register fully: the face, the mill, the troughs, the sieve hall, the drying fields, hard steam, drift, sun-charge, bone ash, copper paddles, oilcloth, raker, brass-crown operator. All post-Unmaking coinages within institutional/trade contexts.
  • The Touched caste vocabulary. Pure, Prime, Second, Third, Feral: a social categorization invented post-Unmaking as the Azure-altered populations became a recognized class. Religious and bureaucratic in origin; folk in everyday use.
  • Watch terminology. Dux, Deputy, Watchman, Captain, station team, watch team. Some of this is consciously revived from old military Latin (dux); some is medieval English (watch, deputy); the combination is a post-Unmaking institutional invention.
  • Mana-handling slang. Keying, seeding, weaving, channeling, projecting, drawing in. The Vizion-trade vocabulary for compulsion and illusion practice. Conclave-formal terms; have spread into common usage among those who work alongside practitioners.
  • Demonyms, and the slang under them. Nyssian, Buralian, Mososi, clipped on the Freeport docks to the Nys, the Burls, the Mos. All three are outsiders’ words: a Nyssian is a Colliman or a Piscean first and says so, and on the Buralian march Buralian is a Freeport usage rather than a local one. The pejoratives are post-Unmaking and each points at a specific grievance: Sellsouls for a country that leases its blight and staffs its mines out of its own courts, ropers and ashmen for the slaving frontier, weedeaters for a people who feed themselves off rafts. Mossies is the exception and is only a sound.
  • Religious vocabulary. Yrt, Father of Earth, Sky, and Sea. The names of the twelve Feral gods (Koya, Kroduk, Shul, Haur, Vexis, Talli, etc.). These are post-Unmaking religious invention. The survivors needed gods, and they made them.
  • Workplace pejoratives. Touched-coded slurs, blight-coded slurs, conclave-coded honorifics that get warped into mockery (Maester in a sarcastic register, Disciple as an insult to a junior worker). Class friction generates vocabulary.
  • Folk-medical vocabulary for things the order does not explain. Drift (per Brusc’s tour: long mana exposure changes how you think, feel, mend). The marrow-deep (replaces pre-Fall limbic with a felt-body description).

Stratification by Class and Institution

Language in Yrt is stratified along three intersecting axes:

Class (literacy)

ClassSpeech profile
Aristocracy / Conclave / Pura Ecclesia leadershipEducated, longer sentences, more formal vocabulary, fewer contractions, deliberate archaisms in ceremonial contexts. Can read pre-Fall texts with effort.
Wealthy merchant class / Brotherhood leadershipPractical, transactional, vocabulary heavy on trade and commerce. Educated but not academic. Reads commercial records and contracts daily.
Skilled trades (Watch, Guild, Mendicants)Mixed: institutional vocabulary in their professional contexts, common speech elsewhere. Many semi-literate (read enough for work, not for pleasure).
Working class (laborers, farmers, charcoal-burners, fishermen)Common speech, contractions, dialect, regional idiom. Largely illiterate. Trade jargon if employed in a specialized industry.
Itinerant / displaced (Touched outsiders, refugees, scavengers)Whatever combination of dialects their wandering has accumulated. Often functional in multiple registers, formal in none.

Institution

  • Conclave register is preserved-formal: deliberate continuity with pre-Fall technical vocabulary, ritualized phrasing, Latinate sound. Cast Conclave Ritual. Arcane Inspection. The Concordat. Sustained channel.
  • Pura Ecclesia register is archaic-formal: thou, thy, inverted constructions, sermon cadence. Hallowed be the Father of Sky. Yrt cleanses the unclean.
  • Brotherhood register is opaque-functional: euphemism, code, deniable phrasing. Verrin doesn’t say “kill”; he says “complete the task.” The criminal register conserves vocabulary too: its phrases are technical to its trade.
  • Town Watch register is military-procedural: short, declarative, vocabulary-conservative. Halt and break. Official escort detachment ready. Sprout a Verdani arrow in your behind.
  • Refiners’ Guild register is working-class but vocabulary-rich within its trade. Honest, direct, mortality-conscious. We don’t talk about Site One.

Region

  • Freeport English is the city dialect: maritime borrowings (rum, aft, draft, lee), commercial idiom, fast-paced speech.
  • Ravenna/blight English is industrial-trade English plus survival-pessimism: short sentences, gallows humor, technical refinery vocabulary woven into everyday speech.
  • Collima/hill English is rural-working: charcoal trade vocabulary, slow cadence, conservative grammar (the old verb forms survive longer in isolation).
  • Pinna mining English has its own miner’s slang the novel hasn’t explored yet.
  • Verdani speech is entirely different: elves speak something with its own grammar, deeply Verdant-coded, and Yrt’s humans don’t generally learn it.

Estimated Current State at 230 SU

  • Spoken common speech has drifted significantly from pre-Fall English. Pronunciation has shifted (not the full Great Vowel Shift, but partial). Slang has invented itself. Lost technology has lost its vocabulary. A modern English speaker dropped into a Stews tavern would understand maybe 60-70% of casual speech.
  • Spoken educated speech has drifted less, because the educated read and write and teach. A modern English speaker dropped into a Conclave classroom would understand 80-90%.
  • Written English of the institutional classes is the most conservative. The Conclave’s published texts, the Pura Ecclesia’s liturgy, the Refiners’ Guild’s training materials, the Brotherhood’s commercial ledgers: all preserve formal English close to (but not identical to) the late-pre-Fall standard. A modern English speaker could read these with light effort.
  • Pre-Fall documents (such as the Manite Introduction from 2041 = Year 29 BU) would feel formal-archaic to a 230 SU reader the way Shakespeare’s English feels to a modern reader: comprehensible with effort, audibly old. Educated readers can parse them; common folk cannot.
